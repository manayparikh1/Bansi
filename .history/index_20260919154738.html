What even is a Bansuri?

A bansuri is a piece of bamboo and it is closed at one end with a blowing hole cut a few centimeters from the end thats closed and that are around 6 to 7 finger holes running down the body of it (the bamboo). That is basically the entire design, so no mechnical keys or any kind of mechanism is added.

Flutists or flautists hold it sideways and they angle it slightly down to the right, and blow across the edge of the hole instead of actually into it. The length of the bamboo decides the pitch of the sound.

From being a cow herdler to performing in a concert hall.

The bamboo flute (bansuri) is so old that nobody can actuall date it. And it is carved into various religious temple walls like Buddhist, Hindu, and Jain. It is super important for Krishna, who is a Hindu god that is depicted for playing the most beautiful flute scales EVER.

Honestly for most of the history it pretty much stayed as a folk instrument. It was small and played in villages and it did not appear on atleast one Hindustani conert stage at all and that all changed one lifetime.

1911-1960

Pannalal Ghosh

He was born in Barisal and he rebuilt the instrument to make it fit for classical music. And it was much more longer than any other flute that time, around 32 inches I believe. It dropped the pitch and gave it the sustain effect a raga needs. He popularized the seventh hole and studied under someone named Allauddin Khan. Before him the bansuri was not even a concert instrument, it was used only for village songs. But he made such a big change that after him it was.

Born on 1938

Hariprasad Chaurasia

He is still alive today and one of my favourite flute players in the world. He carried it out of India. Fifty years of recordings across most of the major ragas. He taught gurukul in Vrindavan, and collabed with Western musicians and he gave the bansuri a profile thats comparable with the sitar.

In a performance the bansuri is usually joined by another Indian instrument called the Tabla, which many of you have heard. It is used for rhythm and a tanpura droning underneath, holding the tonic of the bansuri so the never loses its pitch. You can switch that drone on in the studio below. Try it.

Why having no keys is basically the whole point!

A western flute gives you a key per every note. Press it and you get the note and the pitch between two keys does not exist (some of you may not understand what I am saying here but thats fine). The bansuri has nothing buton open holes and the pads of your fingers, which sounds super good for bansuri because you can add sounds that are not even possible on the western flute by sliding and gliding your fingers. (I'll show you a audio recording of me playing in the very bottom).

Because a finger can cover a hole halfway, or a third of the way, or roll slowly off it, the player has every pitch between the notes. That is how the flats and sharps of different ragas are found on an instrument with holes that are fixed and it is how a bansuri slides from one note into the next into a single breath.

The seven swaras are Sa Re Ga Ma Pa Dha Ni, and Sa is not a fixed frequency surprisingly. It is wherever the flute of the player sits.
